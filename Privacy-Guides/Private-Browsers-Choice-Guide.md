# Take Back Your Browser: A Privacy Guide for the Modern Web

## Introduction

In modern days we spend most of our time connected and web browsers are our portals to access the Internet's world. It is easy to understand that, because we use them to access every service, application or online content, through them a big chunk of our personal data passes.

With this essay I aim to create a simple yet comprehensive guide for everybody, to take back control of your own data and enhance your privacy to safely access the web.

## Why Should I Care About Privacy?

In the new era data are the most important asset, you exist and you are a part of the modern world because there is data that proves that. Every one of us is strictly linked to a set of personal information that identifies us and losing its control is like losing a part of ourselves. Controlling data is equal to controlling the world (Google knows it right?).

Now without thinking about taking the control of the world, everyone should have the right to control their personal data and their virtual persona, being able also to limit the amount that is continuously exchanged and shared on your behalf.

You don't need to be a journalist, an activist or a criminal to care about this. You just need to be a person that values their own security, privacy and digital life.

## ⚠️ The Problem

Big companies have already understood the power that data holds a while ago, so now every application you have installed aims to see and collect a huge variety of your personal data. All this data is not only stored and analyzed by the company that provides the service/software, but very often is also sold to countless third parties for a variety of purposes.

In the case of web browsers not only the program itself can collect your data and spy on you, but also the websites, applications and services that you access are collecting information about you and sending trackers to map your online behavior for commercial purposes and only they know what else.

To give you an idea of the scale: a single visit to a popular news website can trigger requests to **dozens** of third-party domains, each one potentially logging your visit, your device info, your location and your browsing habits. Multiply that by every single page you visit in a day and you start to see the picture.

Also remember that the **Incognito Mode** (or "Private Browsing") on most browsers does not protect you more than the "normal mode". It only clears the cookies and local data of that session after you close it! Your ISP still sees everything, the websites still track you, and your employer's network admin still logs your traffic. It is basically a "don't show my history to the next person who uses this PC" feature, nothing more.

If you started to feel goosebumps don't worry, fortunately there are multiple ways to take back control of a big part of those information and in many cases also stop them from collecting it.

<br/>

### Default Browsers

When you buy a device whether it is a smartphone, a computer, a tablet… it already comes with a web browser installed for you to use, but unfortunately it is almost never the best one in terms of privacy.

The main browsers owned by corporations are just another piece of software to monitor their consumer's behaviors. The most common ones like **Chrome**, **Safari**, **Edge** (to name some), even though they come with a lot of functionalities and are advertised as safe and caring about your privacy, they actually don't do much to preserve your privacy and stop all these trackers. Actually sometimes it is quite the opposite.

A few uncomfortable truths:

- **Google Chrome** sends a significant amount of usage data back to Google. It is tied to your Google account, syncs your browsing habits and feeds the largest advertising machine on the planet. Google's business model *is* your data.
- **Microsoft Edge** has been caught injecting affiliate links into URLs you visit and sends telemetry data to Microsoft by default. It also promotes Bing and Microsoft services aggressively.
- **Apple Safari** is better than the two above in some tracking prevention aspects (Intelligent Tracking Prevention), but it is closed source, locked to the Apple ecosystem, and Apple still collects data tied to your Apple ID.

None of these are "evil" per se, but their incentive structure is not aligned with your privacy. They profit from knowing you.

## The Solution

Ok, all this sounds really bad, so what can we do about it?

Luckily there are still a lot of people that care about privacy and human rights and it happened that many of them are also software developers and have created alternatives for all of us to use. And the best part is that there are **free and open source** options.

You don't need to be a hacker or a Linux wizard. Most of these browsers are easy to install and work just like Chrome or Firefox. You download, you open, you browse. The difference is what happens (or doesn't happen) under the hood.

## 🔍 What to Look For

There are many alternatives to choose from, but what are actually the best ones for a daily use and also the ones that protect me the best without compromising usability? How can I choose the right one for me?

When choosing a web browser there are a few key things you want to check:

- **FOSS (Free and Open Source Software):** The reason why this is important is because open source software has its code publicly available, so anyone can review it and be sure that the program doesn't do any sketchy things behind your back. You don't have to *trust* blindly, you (or someone technical) can *verify*.
<br>

- **Security / Updates:** Considering the rapid growth of threats and technology you surely want a software that is constantly being updated and that brings you new features while also protecting you from the dangers and preserving your privacy. A browser that hasn't been patched in months is a liability, no matter how private it claims to be.
<br>

- **Functionalities and Extensions Availability:** As we have said before, we use the browser for a large amount of different tasks and so we want to have one that has a good amount of built-in features and also the chance to install external plug-ins to fit every need.
<br>

- **Reputation:** It is also important to research before downloading a software to know what is the behavior of the people that provide the software. Are they trustable? (Generally safer when choosing an open source one, as it is community-created software and a single company can't dictate changes silently, but still better to research and be 100% sure about what you are choosing to trust).
<br>

- **Aesthetics and Usability:** Talking about privacy and security this is surely the least important thing, but I personally think that if you have to use something every day you should choose something that looks at least decent and that is easy to use. A browser you hate using is a browser you will eventually abandon, and then you are back to square one.

In conclusion there are also personal preferences. Some browsers may offer very similar performance and features but someone may prefer one over the other. You can have your opinion too and select the one that fits you the most.

## Key Privacy Features to Look For

We have just talked about the importance of having different features and security in place in your browsers. Here I will list some of the most important ones that you will need in order to protect your privacy:

- **Cookies Advanced Settings:** You want to be able to granularly choose what to do with cookies, not only block them or not, but also choosing specific actions/settings for each site (you might have different needs using a different service online). The most common settings to look for and enable here are to block all third-party cookies and also clearing the website data when you close it.
<br>

- **Ads / Tracking Protection:** Websites not only track you with cookies but also in many different other ways to understand your online behavior also on external websites. You want the option to stop all this inside your browser. Look for built-in tracker blocking or at least easy integration with an extension like uBlock Origin.
<br>

- **Fingerprint Protection:** Another way used to track you is by fingerprinting (uniquely identifying) your browser configuration, so they can know that it is still you even with all the other privacy features enabled. This one is more complicated as there are hundreds of different methods in place to try and fingerprint you (canvas, WebGL, audio context, font enumeration, screen resolution, timezone, installed plugins…). Also most aggressive anti-fingerprint protections break websites, but there are still some options available that will protect you while letting you normally surf the web.
<br>

- **HTTPS-Only Mode:** This setting forces all connections to use the encrypted HTTPS protocol to transfer data online, to avoid someone stealing data while it is transmitted. (Nowadays most websites use HTTPS as default, but some still don't, and a forced upgrade protects you on sketchy networks like public Wi-Fi).
<br>

- **Pop-ups and Redirect Blocking:** This is useful because it stops useless ad pop-ups or being redirected to external websites for tracking or other unsafe purposes.
<br>

- **Private Search Engine:** Change the default search engine to one privacy-focused like [DuckDuckGo](https://duckduckgo.com), [Brave Search](https://search.brave.com), [Startpage](https://www.startpage.com), [SearXNG](https://searx.space) (self-hosted meta-search), or [Mojeek](https://www.mojeek.com). These don't build a profile of your queries or feed them to advertisers. -> More on this in the dedicated Search Engines guide.

These are the main important features privacy-related. Of course there is a lot more underground especially in terms of security. I encourage you to research further if you are interested in the topic.

Also for advanced users I suggest to test your browser. There are many different online tests for anti-tracking, anti-fingerprinting, anti-ads:

- [BrowserLeaks](https://browserleaks.com) – comprehensive fingerprint and leak tests
- [Cover Your Tracks (EFF)](https://coveryourtracks.eff.org) – how unique is your browser?
- [AmIUnique](https://amiunique.org) – fingerprint uniqueness check
- [DNS Leak Test](https://www.dnsleaktest.com) – are your DNS queries exposed?

Also checking the source code, `about:config` flags and advanced settings to know better how the software is working and who/what you are trusting.

## 👍 My Recommendations

I understand that while for somebody checking all these things, researching and tweaking is quite fun and interesting, somebody only wants to know what to download to move away from the mainstream browsers.

Here I will give some suggestions. Please remember that these recommendations are the result of my personal opinions and research, I am not sponsored by any of them, and this list is valid at the time of writing which is **mid-2026**. Changes in the market or in those software could modify the situation and hence my recommendations.

<br/>

### For Windows, Mac and Linux

#### Best Out of the Box Browser: [Brave Browser](https://brave.com) / **LibreWolf** [below](#other-good-options)

It is feature-rich, and gives a great protection out of the box. You can also spend time and further tweak it to make it safer and even more private. It has consistently shown some of the best scores in terms of randomizing the fingerprint on multiple test-websites and services.

This browser is free and open source, based on Chromium, so it supports the vast Chrome extension library. It is packed with Brave's own features like Brave Search integration, Brave Wallet (web3/crypto) and an optional paid VPN. You can ignore or disable the crypto stuff if it's not your thing.

> One **note** of transparency: Brave had a small controversy back in 2020 where it was found injecting affiliate codes into some URLs. They addressed it quickly and it hasn't been an issue since, but it is good to know the history. The browser itself remains one of the strongest privacy options with zero configuration needed.

> Lately it got a bit heavy with AI, Wallet, VPN and a bunch of other stuffs, it is still possible to disable everything anyway. They hve also realised a stripped down version calld **Brave Origin** free on Linux.

> **Link:** [https://brave.com](https://brave.com)

<br/>

#### Best for Tor / Dark Web: [Tor Browser](https://www.torproject.org)

This is a bit different. It looks like a normal browser but actually is more than that: it is used to connect to the Tor network, routing your traffic through three encrypted relays around the world.

It is free and open source and has a general good protection in terms of privacy and security. The core is based on the Firefox (Gecko) open source engine. It is available for Linux, Mac, Android and Windows.

> **Link:** [https://www.torproject.org/download/](https://www.torproject.org/download/)

**Note:** Navigating through the Tor network is extremely slow compared to normal browsing. It is not recommended as a daily driver, unless you have some specific needs (journalism, activism, censorship circumvention, accessing .onion services). If you are interested in accessing the Tor network I highly suggest to read and get information about it, there are very good explanations on the [Tor Project website](https://www.torproject.org/about/).

<br/>

#### Other Good Options

- **[LibreWolf](https://librewolf.net):** FOSS, a hardened version of Firefox with privacy-focused defaults baked in. It ships with uBlock Origin pre-installed, disables telemetry, enables strict tracking protection and clears cookies on close by default. If you like Firefox but don't want to spend an hour in `about:config`, this is your pick. If you are a Firefox lover this takes the place of Brave!
  > **Link:** [https://librewolf.net](https://librewolf.net) 
<br>

- **[Mullvad Browser](https://mullvad.net/en/browser):** Made in collaboration between Mullvad VPN and the Tor Project. Very similar to Tor Browser in its anti-fingerprinting approach (all users look the same) but *without* routing through the Tor network, so it is faster. Pairs beautifully with a Mullvad VPN subscription, but works standalone too.
  > **Link:** [https://mullvad.net/en/browser](https://mullvad.net/en/browser)
<br>

- **[DuckDuckGo Browser](https://duckduckgo.com/app):** The privacy search engine provider has built its own browser, now available for Android, iOS, Windows and Mac. It is very simple and clean, with a built-in "Fire Button" to wipe all session data in one tap. It also includes email protection (`@duck.com` addresses) and app tracking protection on mobile. The desktop versions have matured a lot since their initial release but are still more minimal than Brave or Firefox in terms of extension support.
  > **Link:** [https://duckduckgo.com/app](https://duckduckgo.com/app)
<br>

- **[Iridium Browser](https://iridiumbrowser.de):** A Chromium-based FOSS browser focused on stripping out all the Google telemetry and auto-updating phone-home features from Chromium. It is more bare-bone and aimed at users who want the Chromium engine without Google's fingerprints. Updates can sometimes lag behind Chromium releases.
  > **Link:** [https://iridiumbrowser.de](https://iridiumbrowser.de)

There are also some other smaller and new niche browsers (Waterfox, Vivaldi, Ungoogled Chromium…), so if you want to go for one of those you should do some advanced research, as they might have been "less tested" by the wider community or have different trade-offs.

<br/>

#### Best for Mobile: [Brave Browser](https://brave.com/mobile/)

Even on mobile it shares most of the features of the desktop version, showing the best scores in privacy, anti-tracking and anti-fingerprinting protection. It also blocks YouTube ads in the mobile browser, which is a nice bonus.

> **Link:** [https://brave.com/mobile/](https://brave.com/mobile/)

Other good options are:

- **[DuckDuckGo Browser](https://duckduckgo.com/app):** Very basic and fluid interface, offers mail protection and app tracking protection on mobile. Great for people who want "install and forget" simplicity.
<br>

- **[Firefox Focus](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/browsers/mobile/focus/):** A special out-of-the-box hardened version of Firefox for mobile. It blocks trackers by default, always browses in a private session and has a one-tap erase button. Ideally you can also download the regular [Firefox for Mobile](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/browsers/mobile/) but it needs some work to enhance privacy and anti-tracking (install uBlock Origin, change settings).
<br>

- **Firefox Beta**: in the beta release you have access to `about:flags`, can potentially harden it more than *Firefox Focus* which does not support that, bur requires a lot of setup.

<br/>

#### A Note on Chromium and Firefox (Vanilla)

The original [Chromium](https://www.chromium.org) and [Firefox](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/) are FOSS, but they are very bare-bone in terms of advanced privacy controls compared to some of those above and need way more tweaking and extensions to perform similarly in terms of privacy protection and security. They are solid *bases* to build upon, but not great out-of-the-box privacy tools.

If I have missed some in the list it is only because I have put the ones I have personally tested and that are most used, and also most mentioned in the privacy community.

## 🧩 Extensions Recommendations

The browsers of choice in the list already provide good to advanced privacy protections, but it should also be mentioned that you can enhance privacy on many browsers using specific extensions. Some of the most used are:

- **[uBlock Origin](https://github.com/gorhill/uBlock):** Blocks trackers, ads, scripts and more. Free and open source, already packed in LibreWolf. **Important 2026 note:** Due to Chrome's Manifest V3 enforcement, the full uBlock Origin no longer works on Chromium-based browsers (Brave, Chrome, Edge). On those you will get "uBlock Origin Lite" which is less powerful. **On Firefox (and Firefox-based browsers like LibreWolf, Tor Browser) uBlock Origin still works at full power.** This is one of the strongest arguments for choosing a Firefox-based browser in 2026.
<br>

- **[DuckDuckGo Privacy Essentials](https://duckduckgo.com/app):** Blocks some third-party requests and cookies, also sets DuckDuckGo as the default search engine. A good lightweight option for people who don't want to configure much.
<br>

- **[NoScript](https://noscript.net):** A plugin made to stop all scripts in the browser unless you explicitly whitelist them. It is packed in the Tor Browser. Very powerful, but also very aggressive: it will break a lot of websites until you learn to manage the whitelist. Recommended for advanced users.
<br>

- **[Privacy Badger](https://privacybadger.org/)**: a light plugin by the trusted EFF which automatically analyze and block any tracker or ad that violated the principle of user consent. Basically a domain and cookie blocker. No setup required.

Now you can choose one or more and start browsing the web safely without the creepy trackers following you all around.

**Remember:** Installing too many extensions can paradoxically make it *easier* to fingerprint your browser (your unique combination of extensions becomes an identifier). So choose only a few that are reliable and downloaded by many users, so you can better blend in with the crowd.

## Extra Steps

If you want to raise even more your privacy level you can choose to use a **VPN (Virtual Private Network)**. Some of the main advantages are:

- It hides your IP: the websites you visit will see only the VPN's IP address and your ISP would not be able to see anymore your traffic in clear-text (they see an encrypted tunnel to the VPN server, nothing more).
- It encrypts all the traffic before exiting your device, protecting you from MITM (Man-In-The-Middle) attacks, especially on public Wi-Fi.
- Some providers offer also anti-ads/trackers protections within the service (like Mullvad's DNS blocking or Proton's NetShield).
- Remember to choose a safe one, with a **strict no-log policy** (ideally independently audited), strong encryption (WireGuard or OpenVPN with AES-256+), and a favorable jurisdiction (outside the 5/9/14 Eyes alliances if possible).

Some reputable options: [Mullvad VPN](https://mullvad.net) (no account needed, you get a random number, cash/crypto payments), [Proton VPN](https://protonvpn.com) (free tier available, Swiss jurisdiction).

Avoid free VPNs. If you are not paying for the product, *you* are the product. A free VPN that logs and sells your browsing data is worse than no VPN at all.

This is an extra fast and slim explanation on VPNs. If you are interested in the topic i might make a dedicated guide in the future, unsure to check [here](https://github.com/Maat-Cyber/Maat-Cyber-World/Privacy-Guides)

## Disclaimer

Using a private browser doesn't make you some kind of anonymous ghost!

For example they do not hide your IP address, for which you need a VPN, a proxy or using the Tor Browser while connected into the Tor network. Even so these would be only a few of all the steps to try to achieve anonymity, which is a more complicated matter and impossible to get at 100%.

Your browser is one layer. Your OS, your DNS, your email provider, your phone's baseband, the metadata in your photos, your browser *extensions*… they all leak. Privacy is a spectrum and a practice, not a product you install once.

If you are in a country where there is high censorship or you are an activist, a journalist or a whistleblower, I suggest you to take some time and evaluate your **threat model** and move accordingly. This might need a lot of targeted research and actions. Start with the [EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guide](https://ssd.eff.org) and the [Tor Project's documentation](https://tb-manual.torproject.org).

Surf the internet responsibly! 🛡️
